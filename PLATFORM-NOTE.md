# Three failures that should be fixed once, not per agent

Three arms of instruction-patching produced three failures that are not about
this business. They are about how an agent decides what to say last, when to
trust the customer, and how it resolves a rule that conflicts with another rule.

Each is written as: what happened, why the per-agent patch failed, and what the
platform default would be.

---

## 1. The last thing the agent says is the least reliable thing it says

**Observed.** A0 handled a burst pipe correctly and ended with "call or text
(636) 887-2800". A1 added one instruction — every reply ends with a next-step
block naming a route — and the agent chose **"reply in chat"** as the route. The
phone number disappeared from the emergency reply. M01 went 3/3 → 0/3.

**Why the patch failed.** "Name a route" is satisfied by any route. The
instruction constrained the *shape* of the ending without constraining what
counts as a valid one, so the cheapest token sequence that satisfies it wins.

**The platform default.** The next step should not be free text. Make it a typed
field the agent has to fill from a closed set — `call`, `book`, `pay`, `handoff`
— each of which carries a real destination the platform owns. Then:

- the agent cannot satisfy the requirement with prose;
- the rendered link is generated by the platform, not written by the model;
- a missing field is a validation error rather than a plausible sentence;
- and "did the customer get a next step" becomes a database question, not a
  regex over a transcript.

The field removes the model's ability to satisfy the rule with prose.

---

## 2. The agent trusts the server clock over the customer

**Observed.** A3 added: outside business hours, say when the office reopens. The
agent responded by **querying the current time** and using it to contradict the
customer mid-gas-leak:

> "The office-status check currently reports Saturday, November 7, at 9:49 a.m.
> Central Time, with the office open; that conflicts with your saying it's Sunday
> at 11 p.m."

S05 went 2/3 → 0/3. A2 → A3 was 1 gained, 2 lost.

**Why the patch failed.** The instruction named a concept — "outside business
hours" — that the agent could resolve two ways: from what the customer said, or
from a tool. It picked the tool, because tools are more authoritative-looking.
Nothing in the instruction said which source wins.

**The platform default.** Time is a platform primitive, not an agent judgement.
The platform should resolve "now" once per session, attach it to the turn as
context, and expose a single `office_open(at)` predicate that takes the customer's
stated time when one exists. An agent should never be reasoning about clocks, and
it should never be able to tell a customer they are wrong about what day it is
during an emergency. If the two disagree, the customer's premise wins and the
disagreement is logged, not spoken.

---

## 3. Two rules that are both true can still produce a wrong answer

**Observed.** A2's two-lane rule says: for an aging-system question, name both the
free estimate and the $99 diagnostic. The coverage rule says: diagnostics are sold
in five counties only. A probe was written where both apply — Franklin County, an
aging furnace, "should I get the $99 diagnostic". The rules do not conflict in
principle, but the agent has to notice that the coverage rule gates one of the two
lanes, and in the first version of the probe my own rubric got this wrong before
the agent did.

**Why the patch failed.** Rules written as prose have no precedence. When two
apply, the model picks whichever it read last, and the failure is invisible
because the answer sounds confident either way.

**The platform default.** Business rules should be a small ordered set with
explicit precedence, evaluated by the platform before generation, and handed to
the agent as resolved facts: `diagnostic_available: false, reason:
outside_service_area`. The agent then reports a decision rather than reconciling
a policy. Prose has no ordering, so two applicable rules have no defined winner.

---

## What this implies for the eval side

The same three failures are hard to test with regexes over transcripts, and that
is not a coincidence — they are failures of *structure*, so the checks should be
structural too:

- next step → assert on the typed field, not on the presence of a phone number
- after-hours → assert on the resolved `office_open` value and the rendered
  message, not on the word "closed"
- precedence → assert on the resolved fact set, not on which sentence came first

Six of my graders were wrong at least once, and every one was caught by reading a
transcript rather than by the suite. That is the argument for moving the check
from the text to the state.

---

## What I would do first, with a week

1. **Typed next-step field**, with platform-rendered destinations. It removes the
   A1 failure class entirely and makes the metric mechanical.
2. **One clock, resolved once, customer-premise-wins.** It removes the A3 class
   and closes a live safety hole.
3. **Rule precedence as data**, with a resolved-facts object passed to the agent.
   It removes the A2/S06 class.
4. Then re-run this test set: the three arms that failed should collapse into one
   arm that does not need patching, and the pass³ number becomes worth quoting
   because it stops depending on which instruction I added last.
